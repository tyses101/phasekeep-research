What PhaseKeep is, how to read this library, and how we keep ourselves honest

PhaseKeep in one paragraph

Light can carry information in more ways than "on" and "off". A single photon, the smallest piece of light, can be polarized in different directions and can have a twisting wavefront, and each of these properties can hold part of a message. PhaseKeep proposes using two such properties together so that one photon carries one of four addresses, then changing that information with ordinary optical parts, and finally checking that the result is still good before using it again. The proposal rests on two ideas, and everything else in the project is meant to serve them. Underneath both sits one scientific resource, high-dimensional entanglement, described just below.

Key idea

The two pillars.

GEA, Geometric Encoding Axies: a way of organizing the possible states as the corners of a square, cube or higher-dimensional shape, so that "change this one property" becomes a clear step along one edge.

The Buddy System: a way of preparing helper photons and, crucially, checking that an operation worked before continuing, because in optics the signal that says "success" is not always telling the truth.

The cornerstone: high-dimensional entanglement

Under the two pillars sits one scientific foundation, adopted by the project owner: high-dimensional entanglement. Entanglement is a tie between two systems so strong that the pair has a state neither has alone. "High-dimensional" means each system has more than two levels. The first target is two entangled Tetragons, one on each photon: four addresses each, 16 joint states, and up to two ebits of entanglement (an ebit is the entanglement of one maximally entangled pair of two-level systems). Common mistake

Entanglement is a resource, not a result. Highly entangled states can be easy for a classical computer to simulate, and one four-level photon is always easy. A computing advantage would have to be shown by measurement against the best classical method. None has been, and nothing at PhaseKeep has been measured yet. The hard parts are in Volumes 6 to 8: making two photons interact on demand, knowing that it worked, and keeping the result.

Why anyone would try this

A quantum computer's difficulty is rarely the arithmetic. It is keeping fragile information alive through many steps. Light is attractive because photons travel without much disturbance and optical parts are mature technology. Light is difficult because photons almost never interact with each other, so making two photons do something together takes cleverness, and because photons get lost. PhaseKeep is a bet that careful bookkeeping of encoding (GEA) and checking (Buddy) can make an optical approach more manageable. It is a bet, not a result.

First Words

A starting glossary of every background word, for a reader who knows nothing yet

Read this before anything else

This page assumes you know nothing about light, quantum physics or computing. Every word the library leans on is explained here in plain language, in the order a newcomer needs it. Nothing is skipped because it seems obvious. You do not need to memorize any of it. Come back whenever a word stops making sense.

Two things to know first:

  • The words in bold are the ones used later in the volumes. Where a word has a nickname or symbol, it is given.
  • The full project glossary at the back (Appendix 1) lists PhaseKeep's own terms. This page covers the background words those terms are built from.
Key idea

The whole project in six lines. Light comes in tiny pieces called photons. A photon has several properties, such as which way it wiggles and how its beam twists. PhaseKeep proposes using two of those properties together so that one photon carries one of four labels. Ordinary optical parts can then change the label. A separate check decides whether the change worked. Every number in the library says how much we know about it and how we know it.

1. Everyday words used in a special way

  • Information: a choice between possibilities. Which of two switches is on, or which of four labels a photon carries.
  • Bit: the smallest piece of information, a choice between two things, written 0 or 1.
  • State: a full description of how something is set up right now. "The photon's state" means everything about it that we could in principle find out.
  • System: whatever we are describing. A photon, a register or a memory.
  • Model: a set of rules and numbers, usually run on a computer, that imitates how something behaves. A model is only as good as the numbers put into it.
  • Simulation: running a model to see what it predicts. A simulation is not an experiment.
  • Assumption: a number or fact we chose instead of measuring.
  • Measurement (in the lab): reading a value from real equipment. Every PhaseKeep number so far is a calculation or a simulation, not a lab measurement.
  • Classical: ordinary, non-quantum. A classical computer works with bits that are definitely 0 or 1.
  • Resource: anything that is used up or costs something, such as the number of optical parts, the amount of light that survives, or the time taken.

2. Light

  • Light: a wave of electric and magnetic fields that carries energy. It can also be treated as a stream of particles (see photon).
  • Wave: a pattern that repeats as it travels, with crests and troughs, like a ripple on a pond.
  • Wavelength: the distance between two neighbouring crests. It sets the colour of the light.
  • Photon: the smallest piece of light. One photon cannot be split into halves. Single photons are used because quantum effects are easiest to see when you handle one at a time.
  • Bright light, or classical light: a beam with a huge number of photons, such as a laser pointer. It behaves like a smooth wave. Many tests are done with bright light first because it is easier to handle than single photons.
  • Beam: light travelling in a narrow stream.
  • Laser: a source of very pure, steady, single-colour light.
  • Amplitude of a wave: how tall the waves are. A bigger amplitude means a brighter beam. (Quantum amplitudes in section 3 are a related but different idea.)
  • Phase: where in its up-and-down cycle a wave is. Two waves can be the same size but shifted, and that shift is a phase difference.
  • Interference: what happens when two waves overlap. Where crests meet crests the light gets brighter (constructive interference). Where a crest meets a trough it cancels (destructive interference). Much of what follows depends on this.
  • Polarization: the direction in which the light's electric field wiggles, such as up and down or side to side. Polarized sunglasses use it. H means horizontal, V means vertical. There are also diagonal and circular kinds. In circular polarization the wiggle direction turns steadily as the light moves.
  • Wavefront: an imaginary surface joining the crests of a wave. In an ordinary beam it is flat. Picture a sheet moving forward.
  • Orbital angular momentum, OAM, or "twist": some beams have a wavefront that spirals like a corkscrew or a spiral staircase as it travels. The number of turns is a whole number, called the twist, written ℓ (the letter ell). The value 0 is an ordinary beam. A beam with ℓ of +1 spirals one way and −1 spirals the other. A twisted beam has a dark spot in the middle, so it looks like a ring or doughnut.
  • Mode: one particular allowed shape of a beam. "The ℓ = +1 mode" means the beam shape with one turn of twist. Think of modes as separate lanes the light can use.
  • Optical part, or component: any physical piece that handles light. It is not a computer part.
  • Mirror: reflects light. It reverses the direction of a twist, so ℓ = +1 becomes ℓ = −1.
  • Beam splitter: a part that sends part of a beam one way and the rest another way.
  • Polarizing beam splitter: a beam splitter that sorts light by polarization, for example sending H straight on and V off to the side.
  • Waveplate: a slab of crystal that changes the polarization of light passing through it. A half-wave plate, set at the right angle, can turn H into V. A quarter-wave plate turns straight-line polarization into circular and back.
  • Spatial light modulator: a screen-like device that can reshape a beam, including adding a twist.
  • Detector: a device that notices light. A single-photon detector responds to one photon, usually with a click.
  • Loss: light that is absorbed, scattered or sent somewhere we cannot use. Loss is the main enemy in this project.

3. Quantum words

These are the words people find hardest. Take them slowly. Nothing in the library needs more than this.

  • Quantum: the rules that govern very small things, such as photons, atoms and electrons. They differ from everyday experience in the ways below.
  • State (quantum): the full description of a photon's properties. A photon's state can be a definite property, such as "horizontal", or a mixture.
  • Basis state: one of a chosen set of clearly different states that can be told apart perfectly. Horizontal and vertical polarization are two basis states.
  • Superposition: a state that is a blend of basis states, not one or the other. It does not mean the photon is secretly one of them and we do not know which. A diagonal polarization is a superposition of H and V.
  • Amplitude (quantum): a number, which can be positive, negative or complex, that says how much of each basis state is in a blend. The chance of seeing an outcome is the size of its amplitude, squared.
  • Probability: the chance of an outcome. Probabilities of all possible outcomes add up to 1 (or 100 %).
  • Relative phase: the shift between two parts of a blend. Two blends can give exactly the same probabilities and still differ in relative phase, and that difference can change what later operations do. It is the most easily lost piece of information.
  • Measurement: asking a quantum system a question and getting an answer. A measurement gives one outcome and usually changes the state, so the original blend is gone. Reading a state is therefore not the same as copying it.
  • Coherence: the property of keeping a definite relative phase between parts of a blend. Losing it is called decoherence. A memory or a beam path is good if it preserves coherence.
  • Qubit: a quantum system with two basis states. The quantum version of a bit.
  • Qudit: a quantum system with d basis states. A system with four is a four-level system, sometimes called a ququart.
  • Dimension: the number of basis states. A qubit has dimension 2 and the Tetragon has dimension 4.
  • Entanglement: a link between two separate things such that the pair has a state that cannot be written as a separate state for each one.
  • High-dimensional entanglement: entanglement between systems that each have more than two levels. Two entangled four-level systems, for example, have 16 joint states.
  • Ebit: the unit of entanglement. One ebit is the entanglement of one maximally entangled pair of two-level systems. Two maximally entangled four-level systems hold 2 ebits, because the count for equal weights is the base-2 logarithm of the number of levels.
  • Hyperentanglement: entanglement in more than one property of the same two photons at once, such as polarization and twist.
  • Separable and non-separable: a joint state is separable if it can be written as one state for the first property times one state for the second. If not, it is non-separable. We use these words for two properties of one photon. The word entangled is used for two separate photons.
  • Schmidt weights: two numbers that tell you how separable a two-part state is. Weights of 1 and 0 mean fully separable. Weights of 0.5 and 0.5 mean the most non-separable a two-level pair can be.
  • Fidelity: a score from 0 to 1 (0 % to 100 %) for how close a state is to the one we wanted. 1 is perfect. Fidelity says nothing about how much light survived. That is a separate number.
  • Reversible, or unitary: an operation that loses no information, so it can in principle be run backwards. All the "perfect" operations in the math are unitary. Filtering and measuring are not.
  • No-cloning: an unknown quantum state cannot be copied exactly. A recipe that you know can be repeated. A state that you do not know cannot be duplicated.
  • Photon number: how many photons are in a mode. The vacuum means none.
  • Noise photons, or thermal photons: unwanted photons that arrive in a mode and spoil the state. The average number of them is written n̄ (n-bar) later on.

4. Words for how PhaseKeep encodes information

  • Encoding: choosing which physical property stands for which label.
  • Encoding Axis (EA, plural Axies): one physical property chosen to carry part of a label, such as polarization or twist. "Axis" because, like a map axis, it has a set of distinguishable values along it.
  • Address: the label of one basis state. On the Tetragon the addresses are 00, 01, 10 and 11.
  • Register: an information-carrying system with a defined set of states, such as the group of photon properties that together hold one address.
  • Tetragon (PK-Q4): our four-address register, made from one photon and two properties. The first digit of an address is the polarization (0 means H, 1 means V). The second digit is the twist (0 means ℓ = −1, 1 means ℓ = +1). So 00 is H with twist −1, 01 is H with twist +1, 10 is V with twist −1 and 11 is V with twist +1.
  • Geometric Encoding Axies (GEA): drawing the addresses as the corners of a shape. Two binary properties give a square, three give a cube, and more give a hypercube. Changing one property moves you along one edge.
  • Corner, or vertex: one address on the shape.
  • Edge, or line: two corners that differ in one property only, for example 00 and 01.
  • Diagonal: two corners that differ in two properties, for example 00 and 11.
  • Face: a flat piece of the shape, such as a square within a cube.
  • Hypercube: the same shape in more than three dimensions. You cannot picture it, but you can count its corners, edges and faces by formula.
  • Subspace: the set of states you can build out of a chosen few addresses. An edge gives a two-address subspace and a square gives a four-address one.
  • Edge blend and diagonal blend: a mix of two corners joined by an edge, or by a diagonal. The diagonal kind is non-separable. The edge kind is not.

5. Words for changing and checking

  • Operation, or gate: a step that changes a state. In optics it is made by a part such as a waveplate or a mirror.
  • Controlled Transformation (CT): a physical operation that we can set and repeat that changes the encoded information. "Controlled" means we can adjust it, not that it depends on another photon.
  • Conditional operation, or controlled gate: an operation that happens only if another system is in a certain state. Making this work between two photons is hard, because photons ignore each other.
  • Closure: whether an operation keeps the photon inside the set of states we are using. An operation that sends it to some other state is not closed on our set.
  • Ancilla, or helper photon: an extra photon, not part of the answer, that is used to make an operation work or to check that it worked.
  • Herald, or heralded: a detector click that is supposed to announce that an operation succeeded. Heralded means success is announced this way. Detection is not the same as verification, because the click can fire when the output is actually wrong.
  • Post-selection: keeping only the runs where a chosen detector outcome occurred, and throwing the rest away. It makes results look better than the success rate really is, so the discarded runs have to be counted.
  • Success probability, or yield: the fraction of attempts that work. For a probabilistic operation this can be small, such as 1 in 8.
  • Buddy System: the PhaseKeep idea of preparing helper photons and checking each operation before going on.
  • Buddy Check: a test with three honest answers. PASS means there is demonstrated evidence it worked. FAIL means there is demonstrated evidence it did not. UNKNOWN means we cannot tell, and that is the default.
  • Certification, or verification: proving that a state or result is what we think it is, ideally without using up the photons.
  • Compiler, or controller: a classical program that decides which supported operations to apply and in what order. It is ordinary software.

6. Words for memory and loss

  • Quantum memory: a device that stores a photon's state and gives it back later. Keeping the relative phase is the hard part.
  • Atomic vapor, or vapour, memory: a memory built from a cell of atomic gas. Light is absorbed into the atoms and later released.
  • Transmission, or efficiency: the fraction of light that comes through a part or a whole chain. 98 % means 2 % was lost. Losses along a chain multiply.
  • Survival: the fraction of photons that are still there at the end. It is reported separately from fidelity, since a perfect state that rarely arrives is not much use.
  • Threshold: the level a number must beat before something is worthwhile. For a memory, the threshold is the efficiency below which a simple measure-and-resend method does as well.
  • Classical benchmark, or bound: the best score a non-quantum method can reach on the same task. A quantum device earns credit only for beating it.
  • Chain: all the stages a photon passes through, from source to detector. The overall efficiency is the product of the stages.
  • Reliability (of a herald): of the runs where the herald fires, the fraction in which the output is actually valid.

7. Words and symbols for reading the math

You will see a few symbols. None of them needs more than this explanation. Appendix 4 has a longer, gentle refresher.

  • Ket: a way of writing a state, with a vertical bar on the left and an angle bracket on the right. The ket for address 01 is written with 01 between them. It just means "the state called 01".
  • √2 (square root of 2): a blend of two equal parts has each part multiplied by 1 over √2 (about 0.707), so that the probabilities, which are the squares, add to 1.
  • ⊗ (tensor product): "combine these two parts into one joint system". It is how a polarization state and a twist state form one photon's state.
  • i: the imaginary unit, a number whose square is −1. It appears because phase shifts are written as complex numbers.
  • Matrix: a grid of numbers that describes an operation on a state. A 4 × 4 matrix acts on a four-level state.
  • Unitary: see section 3. The matrices for ideal operations are unitary.
  • %: a percentage. 56.9 % means 0.569.
  • n̄, F, η: n̄ is the average noise photon number, F is fidelity, and η (eta) is a fraction of light that passes, an efficiency.

8. The honesty labels

Every number and claim in the library has one of these labels. They are explained in Start Here.

  • DERIVED: worked out by mathematics from stated assumptions.
  • REPORTED: a published source says so, and we say how much of it we read.
  • SIMULATED: the output of a model.
  • ASSUMED: a number we chose.
  • PROPOSED: an idea, not shown to work.
  • MEASURED: measured by PhaseKeep in a lab. None yet.
  • UNVERIFIED: supplied or remembered but not checked.
  • BLOCKED: cannot move forward without something we lack.
In plain words

If you remember only one thing: a nice-looking number is worth only as much as the label next to it. Please check the label.

Volume 1

Light, Waves and Photons

Polarization, interference, and the twisting beams called OAM

What you will learn

By the end of this volume you can say what a photon is, what polarization is, what a "twist" of light is, and why a single photon can carry a small label made from both. No math is needed for the main text.

1.1 The photon

Light comes in the smallest possible pieces called photons. A dim laser beam is a stream of them. A single-photon source delivers them one at a time. One photon cannot be split in half by ordinary optics: it goes one way or the other, or, as Volume 2 explains, it can be in a blend of ways until it is measured.

In plain words

A photon is one indivisible packet of light. We care about it because it can carry a label (what we call information) in the shape of its wave, and it travels fast and far with little disturbance.

1.2 Light is a wave, too

The same photon behaves like a wave. A wave has a direction it travels, a size (brightness, which for one photon means the probability of finding it), and a shape across the beam. Two shape properties matter here: which way the wave wiggles (polarization) and how its wavefront is arranged around the beam axis (twist).

1.3 Polarization: which way the light wiggles

Light wiggles sideways to its direction of travel. If it wiggles up and down it is vertically polarized (V). If it wiggles left and right it is horizontally polarized (H). Polarized sunglasses block one of these.

Two points make polarization useful as a label:

  • H and V are completely distinguishable. A polarizing beam splitter sends H one way and V the other, so one measurement tells them apart.
  • A waveplate (a slice of special crystal) changes polarization on purpose. A half-wave plate set at 45 degrees swaps H and V. That is a controlled, repeatable change and it is cheap, mature technology.
Go deeper (optional math)

A half-wave plate at 45 degrees acts on the polarization qubit as the matrix −iX, where X swaps H and V. The factor −i is a global phase when the plate acts alone, which cannot be observed, but it matters if the operation is applied to only part of a larger superposition. We checked this closure: two quarter-wave plates in a row make one half-wave plate DERIVED.

1.4 Twist: orbital angular momentum

Most laser beams have a flat wavefront like a sheet of paper. A beam can instead have a wavefront shaped like a spiral staircase or a corkscrew going around the beam axis. Such a beam has orbital angular momentum (OAM), and its twist number is written ℓ (the Greek letter ell, a whole number such as −1, 0, +1).

  • ℓ = 0 is the ordinary flat beam.
  • ℓ = +1 goes once around the axis, one way, per wavelength travelled.
  • ℓ = −1 goes once around, the other way.
  • A beam with ℓ ≠ 0 has a dark spot at the center, so its cross-section looks like a doughnut.
Go deeper (optional math)

An ideal OAM mode has angular dependence exp(iℓφ), where φ is the angle around the axis, and carries orbital angular momentum ℓℏ per photon DERIVED. The common practical family is the Laguerre-Gaussian modes. With radial index p = 0 and an aperture of radius R, the fraction of power passing through is the regularized gamma function P(|ℓ|+1, 2R²/w²) for beam size w, which is what the A06 simulation used DERIVED; the aperture effect is why larger |ℓ| beams are harder to handle in small optics.

Why use ℓ = −1 and ℓ = +1 for the label? They are mirror images of each other. They are the simplest pair of twisted beams that are each other's opposites. A mirror flips one into the other with a single reflection (ℓ becomes −ℓ), and the pair stays closed under that flip, which is a useful property for a four-address register.

Common mistake

Twist is not spin. Polarization is sometimes called spin angular momentum of light. OAM is about the beam's spatial shape. They are separate properties, which is exactly why they can be combined. The one place they can be linked is a device called a q-plate, which ties them together. In this library the Tetragon treats them as two independent labels.

1.5 Two labels on one photon

Polarization has two options (H or V). Twist, as we use it, has two options (−1 or +1). A photon can carry both at once, giving 2 × 2 = 4 combinations. That is the idea behind the four-address photon introduced in Volume 3.

PropertyA typical way to read itHow clean is it
Polarization H/VPolarizing beam splitter, then two detectorsVery clean; standard

Evidence status

Evidence status. Polarization and OAM definitions, and the half-wave-plate and mirror facts: DERIVED and standard physics. Which sorter PhaseKeep would use is PROPOSED and is audit item A02 in Volume 9. The sorter used in our computer model is illustrative only ASSUMED; published sorters are different devices.

Try it

  1. A beam is in state V. You pass it through a half-wave plate at 45 degrees. What do you get? (Answer: H.)
  2. A photon has twist +1. It reflects off one mirror. What twist does it have? (Answer: −1.)
  3. How many different labels can a photon carry if it uses polarization only? Polarization plus twist (±1)? (Answer: 2, then 4.)

Summary

Key idea

A photon can carry a polarization label (H or V) and a twist label (−1 or +1) at the same time. Both can be changed with ordinary optics, and both can be read out. Together they give four addresses.

Volume 2

States, Measurement and Why Reading Is Hard

Superposition, amplitudes, phase, fidelity, and the no-cloning rule

What you will learn

What a quantum state is, why "blend" is not a joke, why one measurement cannot reveal an unknown state, and why that last fact matters for everything that follows.

2.1 One photon, many possibilities

Ask a photon with polarization H which way it is polarized and it says H, every time. Ask a photon in V, and it says V. Quantum physics allows something else: a photon whose polarization is a blend of H and V. This is called a superposition.

A blend is not "H or V but we do not know which". Experiments show that the two possibilities can interfere with each other, the way two ripples on a pond combine. So the blend is a real thing with its own properties.

2.2 Amplitudes and probabilities

We describe the blend with two numbers, called amplitudes, one for each possibility. Write the state as

state = a × H + b × V

The chance of finding H when you measure is the square of the size of a, and the chance of finding V is the square of the size of b. These chances add to one.

In plain words

Example: a = b = 0.7071. Each chance is 0.7071² = 0.5. A measurement gives H half the time and V half the time. The photon was not secretly one or the other beforehand; the blend itself has this 50/50 property.

2.3 Phase: the part people forget

Amplitudes can be negative or even complex. Two blends with the same probabilities can still be different states:

  • (H + V) / √2
  • (H − V) / √2

Measuring H or V gives 50/50 for both. But a different measurement (diagonal polarization) tells them apart perfectly. The sign is called the relative phase, and it is the very thing that quantum memories and quantum gates must preserve.

Key idea

Probabilities tell you only part of a state. The relative phases between the amplitudes are equally real, and they are the first thing noise destroys.

2.4 One measurement cannot reveal an unknown state

If someone hands you a photon in an unknown blend and you measure it, you get one result (H or V). The blend is gone. You learn a single bit about a state that needed more numbers to describe. Repeating the measurement on the same photon does not help, because after the first measurement the photon is no longer in the blend.

To learn an unknown state you would need many identical copies. But there is a theorem, the no-cloning theorem, that says you cannot make perfect copies of an unknown quantum state. So an unknown state cannot be "backed up" the way a computer file can.

Common mistake

Common mistake. "Four outcomes means four bits." Four addresses (as in Volume 3) return only two bits of ordinary information when read out, and a single reading returns just one address. The state (all the amplitudes and phases together) is richer than any one reading, but you cannot read it all out in one go.

This matters in two ways later in the library:

  1. A known recipe can be rebuilt. If we know how a state was prepared, we can prepare it again from scratch.
  2. An unknown state that was the result of a computation cannot be re-made if lost. This is why keeping it alive (memory, Volume 8) and checking it without destroying it (Buddy Checks, Volume 7) are central problems.

2.5 A four-level system

One photon with two polarizations and two twists can be in a blend of four addresses. We write its state as

state = a|00⟩ + b|01⟩ + c|10⟩ + d|11⟩

where the squared sizes of a, b, c, d add to one. Volume 3 says what each address means. Here we only need the counting.

Go deeper (optional math)

A pure state of a d-level system has d complex amplitudes. Normalization removes one real number and the unobservable global phase removes another, so it takes 2d − 2 real parameters to specify it DERIVED. For d = 2 that is 2 (the two angles on a sphere), for d = 4 it is 6.

2.6 How good can a classical copy be?

Suppose a state is chosen at random and handed to you once. You measure it, then build a new photon from your result. How closely does the new photon match the original, on average? For random pure states in d dimensions the best average match, called fidelity, from this measure-and-rebuild approach is 2/(d+1) DERIVED (a standard result). For d = 2 it is 2/3. For our d = 4 it is 2/5 = 0.40.

So a quantum memory that returns a hidden random four-address state with fidelity above 0.40 is doing something a classical measure-and-resend device cannot. Different tests use different benchmarks, and Volume 8 explains why we keep them apart.

Try it

Check the formula. For d = 2, the benchmark is 2/(2+1). What is it? (2/3, about 0.667.) For d = 4? (2/5 = 0.40.) What happens as d grows? (The benchmark falls toward zero, so a larger system is easier to certify as "nonclassical" but harder to build.)

2.7 What "fidelity" means

Fidelity is a score from 0 to 1 for how close one quantum state is to another. 1 means identical. A fidelity of 0.9 does not mean "90% of photons survive"; survival and state quality are separate numbers, and mixing them up is one of the most common errors in the field. We report them separately everywhere in this library.

Key idea

Encoding Axies and the Tetragon

How a single photon carries four addresses

What you will learn

What an Encoding Axis is, how two of them make a four-address photon (the Tetragon), and what the word "address" does and does not mean.

3.1 What is an Encoding Axis?

An Encoding Axis (EA) is one physical property of light that we choose to carry a label, where the possible values are clearly distinguishable. (The plural is written Axies in the project's own spelling; "Axes" is the same word.)

Polarization is an EA with two values, H and V. Twist is an EA with two values, −1 and +1. Other properties of light could in principle be EAs too: which path the photon takes, its color (frequency), or the radial shape of its beam. Those are candidates, not adopted.

Key idea

Time is not an EA here. Time is the machine's clock: it tells us when a photon arrives. We do not use it as a label in the current design. An earlier memory model used four time slots as the label (Volume 8); the ORCA memory paper's abstract reports storing and retrieving four independent time-bin modes. The current design treats time as external. This library does not record why that choice was made.

3.2 The Tetragon: two axes, four addresses

The Tetragon (formal name PK-Q4) combines polarization (H/V) with twist (−1/+1).

Read each address as two binary digits: the first says polarization (0 is H, 1 is V), the second says twist (0 is −1, 1 is +1).

3.3 A state, not just an address

One photon is not forced to sit on one corner. It can be in a blend of all four:

a|00⟩ + b|01⟩ + c|10⟩ + d|11⟩

with the squared sizes of a, b, c, d adding to one, and with phases that matter (Volume 2). Measuring it returns one address with probability |a|², |b|², |c|² or |d|².

In plain words

Two kinds of blend. A blend of two addresses on one edge of the square, such as 00 + 01, can be written as one polarization times one twist blend. A blend across a diagonal, such as 00 + 11, cannot: its polarization and twist are tied together. Volume 4 explains why that matters.

Common mistake

An address is not a bit-string you can read in full. One detection gives one address. The blend, with all its phases, is only available as long as the photon is not measured.

3.4 Why bother with a second axis?

Compare the options for a single photon:

The attraction is more states per photon without needing more photons, and the fact that polarization and twist can be changed independently by different devices. The price is that twist devices are less mature than polarization ones.

3.5 What can go wrong

  • Crosstalk: a twist sorter sends some of the −1 light to the +1 output, and the reverse.
  • Loss: each device loses some light, and high-twist beams are harder to pass through small optics.
  • Closure: a device that was meant to flip −1 and +1 might also push light into twist 0 or +3. A device is useful only if it keeps the photon inside the four addresses ("closure"). In Volume 5 we check this device by device.
Go deeper (optional math)

Geometric Encoding

Squares, cubes and hypercubes as a map of states

What you will learn

How to draw the possible states of a register as a shape, why the shape is a square, a cube, or a higher-dimensional cube, how to count its corners, edges and faces, and why "an edge on the drawing" is not always "a cheap step in the lab".

4.1 From labels to a shape

Take one binary EA. It has two values, so draw two dots and one line between them. Add a second binary EA: now two copies of that line, joined corner to corner, forming a square. Four dots, four edges. This square is the Tetragon of Volume 3.

Add a third binary EA and you get a cube. A fourth gives a four-dimensional cube (a tesseract), which we cannot draw but can count.

GEA (Geometric Encoding Axies) is the project's name for reading the state space this way. A corner is an address. An edge joins two addresses that differ in exactly one axis, so an edge means "change just that one property". A face (a square) means "two axes together".

4.4 What an edge means, and what it costs

On the drawing, every edge looks the same. In a lab, they do not cost the same. Flipping polarization is one waveplate. Flipping twist might be a mirror, or a hologram, or a sorter. Moving to a state that differs in several axes needs several devices in a row.

Common mistake

The map is not the territory. Geometry tells you which states are neighbours in the description. It does not tell you that stepping between them is cheap, accurate or lossless. That is what audit item A06 (Volume 9) set out to measure.

4.5 What the shape does not give you

  • It does not give a speedup. The cube lists addresses. It says nothing on its own about doing a calculation faster than a normal computer.
  • A pure state is not a point on the cube. A state is a blend over all the corners with phases. The corners are the basis; the state lives in the space they span. For d corners it takes 2d − 2 real parameters DERIVED.
  • More corners need more distinct, stable devices. Each axis added is another property to prepare, change and read.

4.6 Shared or individual changes: the A06 question

Suppose we want to swap H and V on a photon that may be in any of several twist modes, leaving each mode in place. Do we do it in one shared operation (one half-wave plate acts on every mode at once), or do we single out each mode, change it separately, then recombine? The second route needs a sorter before and an inverse sorter after.

A computer model (A06) compared the two for N = 2 (the Tetragon pair), 4, 8, 16 and 32 modes. All error and loss numbers in it are assumptions ASSUMED, so the result describes the model, not a lab SIMULATED. In plain terms:

4.7 Four kinds of geometry

The word "geometry" is used for four different things in PhaseKeep, and only two of them are physical. Keeping them apart avoids a lot of confusion.

GeometryMathematical or physical?What it describes

Optical-mode geometryPhysicalThe actual intensity, phase and polarization pattern of the light across the beam
Execution geometryPhysicalThe arrangement of components, light paths and interaction points on the bench

The photon is not shaped like a square. The square describes how its possible encoded states relate to each other. But those states belong to real properties of light, so the mathematics can have physical consequences, which is what the next section shows.

A research question follows: how well can these four geometries be made to complement one another? If a mathematical relationship matches something light does naturally, the operation may be cheap. If it does not, we pay for sorters and extra parts. The A06 simulation above is one narrow example: swapping H and V does the same thing to every twist mode, so one shared plate does it. Whether this idea gives a general advantage is not established PROPOSED.

A state that can be written as one polarization times one twist blend is called separable; one that cannot is non-separable. The diagonal blend is non-separable: its polarization and twist are correlated inside a single photon, with no second photon involved. For this state the light's polarization goes through both diagonal linear polarizations and both circular ones, repeating twice per turn around the beam axis. Common mistake

Non-separable is not the same as "structured". A separable edge blend also produces a structured beam: its intensity has two lobes, with uniform polarization. Non-separability matters specifically for the coupling between polarization and twist, not for structured light in general.

Two facts follow, both checked numerically DERIVED:

  • Applying an independent unitary operation to each axis, such as a waveplate and a mirror, cannot change the Schmidt weights. A separable state stays separable and a non-separable one stays non-separable.
  • Creating or undoing non-separability needs a coupled operation, such as a q-plate-type device or an interferometer. Filtering and measurement are separate cases that change the weights and are not covered by this rule.

4.10 Geometry as a way to describe operations

So far we have used the shape to organize addresses. A sharper question is whether the shape can describe operations on the state space. Each geometric feature picks out a subspace: the set of states that can be built from the addresses it contains.

For the 12-axis register of Section 4.2 the counts are: 4,096 vertices (dimension 1), 24,576 lines (2), 67,584 squares (4), 112,640 cubes (8) and 126,720 tesseracts (16) DERIVED. These subspaces overlap. They are different ways of addressing parts of one state space, not independent processors.

Four points keep this honest:

  • A line is not a path. A drawn edge does not show the route a state takes. It marks a two-dimensional subspace inside which the amplitudes and relative phase can change continuously.
  • "Isolate" is not a reversible operation. Selecting a two-address subspace by filtering or projecting throws the rest away and costs light. Swapping, mixing and phase changes within the subspace are reversible; isolating is not.
  • Parallel lines save parts, not steps. If both OAM exchanges (00↔01 and 10↔11) are the same physical action, one mirror does both. That is what A06 exploited for the complementary case, a polarization exchange across many twist modes. The saving is in hardware; it is not a quantum speedup.
  • The geometry does not say which operations exist. Volume 5 lists swap and phase operations for the twist axis. A general mixing of twist −1 with +1 is not on that list, and which device would do it is an open question PROPOSED.

The diagonal case is worth testing with numbers. Rotating address 00 toward 11 by 45° gives (00 + 11)/√2, with Schmidt weights 0.5 and 0.5: non-separable. Flipping both axes together (X on each) just turns 00 into 11, which is still separable DERIVED. So selectively mixing a diagonal pair can create the tie between the axes, while a global double flip cannot. The program tetragon.py checks this.

4.11 PQGS: a name for what we study

Photonic Quantum Geometric Superpositions (PQGS) is a descriptive PhaseKeep term for coherent photonic superpositions organized within a geometrically structured encoding space, with particular interest in non-separable states that span more than one Encoding Axis.

  • It is a research term inside GEA, not a new pillar and not a new physical phenomenon.
  • "Geometric" means the organization of the addresses. It does not mean geometric phase (the Berry or Pancharatnam-Berry phase) or geometric quantum computation, which are different, established topics. For example, the abstract of Marrucci, Manzo and Paparo (Phys. Rev. Lett. 96, 163905) says that converting spin angular momentum to orbital angular momentum in such media is associated with Pancharatnam-Berry geometrical phases REPORTED (abstract only).
  • The underlying superposition mathematics is standard. Any claim that this organization makes states easier to prepare, transform or preserve is PROPOSED and untested.

What you will learn

5.1 What "CT" means here

A Controlled Transformation is a physical operation that we can set and repeat, and that changes the encoded information in a known way.

Common mistake

"Controlled" means adjustable, not conditional. In quantum computing, "controlled" often means "do this only if another qubit is 1". Here it simply means we control the device: we choose its setting. A CT is not automatically conditioned on another qubit. When we mean the conditional kind, we say "controlled on" something, and Volume 6 covers it. The older working terms PTEA and TV are retired; CT replaced both.

5.3 Phase is the quiet operation

Most of the useful single-photon operations are phase changes. They do not change how likely each address is, but they change how the addresses interfere. Because the two Tetragon axes are independent, a polarization operation and a twist operation can be applied separately and in either order. Such independent operations also cannot create or remove the tie between polarization and twist (Volume 4, section 4.8); only a coupled device can.

Go deeper (optional math)

In matrix form, half-wave plate at 45 degrees acts on the polarization qubit as −iX (X is the H/V swap). A mirror acts on the twist qubit as X, up to a phase convention. A relative phase of exp(−2iφ) between twist +1 and −1 acts as a rotation about the Z axis of the twist qubit by angle 2φ. Because the two axes are independent, a joint operation is the tensor product of the two DERIVED.

5.4 Shared or individually addressed

One waveplate acts on every part of the beam at once. If we want to change only one address, we first need to separate the addresses (a sorter), change one, and recombine. Volume 4 described the A06 comparison, and Volume 9 gives the results. The lesson for design: a shared operation is the cheap one, and operations that require singling out an address pay for the sorter.

Two Photons: Making Light Interact

Why photons ignore each other, and how a heralded gate fakes it

What you will learn

Why photons do not normally affect each other, what a controlled-SUM operation is, how a published experiment forced two photons to interact (by throwing away most attempts), what this means for how many such operations we would need, and what two entangled Tetragons look like (the project's first target).

6.1 The problem: photons ignore each other

Two flashlight beams cross and pass through each other unchanged. Single photons are the same: they do not interact. That is wonderful for carrying information and terrible for computing, because computing needs one piece of information to change another.

6.2 Controlled-SUM: the two-register operation we want

Treat each photon as a four-level register with addresses 0, 1, 2, 3. Controlled-SUM takes two registers (a control j and a target k) and replaces the target by the sum modulo 4:

|j⟩|k⟩  becomes  |j⟩|(j + k) mod 4⟩

It is the four-level version of the familiar CNOT. Facts we verified numerically DERIVED:

  • SUM can be built from a diagonal controlled-phase operation CP, which multiplies each |j,k⟩ by i^(jk), sandwiched between a Fourier transform and its inverse on the target: SUM = (I ⊗ F†)·CP·(I ⊗ F).
  • The phase i^(jk) can itself be split into three two-bit phase gates: two controlled-Z and one controlled-S.
  • Kickback. If the target is prepared in the right Fourier state |B_r⟩, then SUM leaves the target alone and applies a phase Z^(−r) to the control. This is a way to apply an operation to one photon using another as a tool.

6.3 How many "real" interaction gates would we need?

An operator Schmidt rank measures how far a two-register operation is from being a product of one operation on each register. Rank 1 means it is a product. A higher rank means more interaction is needed to build it, and the rank sets a floor on the number of interaction gates in a stated gate model (below). It is a mathematical count. It is not a measure of physical interaction strength, and it is not the same as the power to create entanglement (section 6.7).

OperationOperator Schmidt rank
CP on four-level registers4
One controlled-phase-flip (CPF, flips the sign of the single state 3,3 only)2

A circuit of m CPF gates (with free single-photon operations in between) can reach rank at most 2^m. CP has rank 4, so at least 2 CPF gates are needed DERIVED. This is a lower bound. The true number needed is undefined until someone finds a circuit. BLOCKED on that search.

The bound belongs to a stated gate model. If every available interaction gate has operator Schmidt rank at most r, a target of rank R needs at least the smallest whole number k with r^k at least R. It is a floor, not a prediction, and it is often not tight. Example: a SWAP of two two-level systems has rank 4 and a CNOT has rank 2, so the floor is two CNOTs, yet the standard exact circuit uses three DERIVED, checked by two_tetragons.py. It also says nothing directly about the number of optical components.

Go deeper (optional math)

The rank of a diagonal two-register operator is the matrix rank of its 4 × 4 table of phases. For CP the table is the Fourier matrix, which has full rank 4. For CPF the table equals the all-ones matrix with one entry flipped, which has rank 2.

6.4 Making photons talk: the published gate

Liu et al. (arXiv 2407.16356, a July 2024 preprint, not peer reviewed at the time we read it) report a heralded four-dimensional controlled-phase-flip gate using orbital angular momentum REPORTED. In plain terms:

  • Two photons are the computational photons. Two more are auxiliary helpers.
  • The conditional phase is produced optically, and the helpers are measured to announce success.
  • The gate works only when specific detectors fire. The theoretical success rate is 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8.
  • Success is judged by post-selection: they keep runs where exactly one photon appears at each output port, and a Bell-state measurement on the helpers' polarization is part of the herald. Only one outcome (Φ⁺) is described experimentally.
  • Reported process fidelity is 0.64 to 0.82, measured using four-photon coincidences.
  • It flips the sign of only |3,3⟩.
Key idea

Heralded means: a detector click is supposed to announce success, so you know when to trust the output. Post-selected means: you keep the runs that look right and discard the rest. The gate works, but only on the runs you kept.

6.5 Why bunching makes a gate

When two identical photons meet at a balanced beam splitter, they leave together: |1,1⟩ becomes (|2,0⟩ − |0,2⟩)/√2, with both photons in the same port DERIVED (sign convention dependent). That "stick together" behaviour is a two-photon effect that depends on whether the photons are in the same mode, so it can be used to attach a conditional phase.

6.7 Two Tetragons: entanglement between registers

So far one photon carried one register. Now put a Tetragon on each of two photons, A and B. Each has four addresses, so together they have 4 × 4 = 16 joint states. This is high-dimensional entanglement: entanglement between systems with more than two levels each.

The simplest example is the equal blend of "both photons at 00", "both at 01", "both at 10" and "both at 11". Each photon alone looks completely random, yet the two always agree. This state has four equal Schmidt weights of 0.25, which is the most entanglement two four-level systems can hold: 2 ebits (an ebit is the entanglement of one maximally entangled pair of two-level systems). The pairing of A's addresses with B's can follow different patterns. Take address k = 2 × polarization + twist, so 0, 1, 2, 3 are 00, 01, 10, 11:

  • The first four are each a polarization pair times a twist pair: one pair entangled in polarization and one in twist, on the same two photons. Moving between them needs only a separate operation on each axis of photon B.
  • The cyclic rotation is not such a product. Reaching it needs an operation inside photon B that couples its polarization and its twist DERIVED. In fact it is an exchange of the two axes (a SWAP: the polarization value becomes the twist value and the other way round) followed by a flip of one axis. Checked by two_tetragons.py.

This suggests sorting operations by what they need, not by how simple they look on the drawing:

So "needs coupled control" does not mean "creates entanglement", and operator rank is a statement about whether the operation factors, not about physical interaction strength. Two operations that look equally simple as geometry can need very different equipment. Whether a device for the axis SWAP exists, and at what loss, is an open question for Volume 5 PROPOSED. A compiler could use this sorting to choose between plans for the same task, but nobody has shown that it saves anything. The five patterns are different target states, not different routes to one goal, so their costs cannot be compared with each other as if they were.

What we can and cannot do to the entanglement

  • Relabel it. Local operations on one photon move the pattern around without changing the amount (above).
  • Not raise it with local operations. Increasing entanglement takes an interaction between the photons or an extra entangled resource. This is a standard result of quantum information theory.
  • Change a joint phase carefully. A sign change that involves both photons can change entanglement while leaving every measurement probability the same, exactly as in Volume 4 section 4.8. "Changing a phase" is therefore not automatically harmless to the entanglement DERIVED.

The Buddy System and Buddy Checks

Detection is not verification

What you will learn

Why a detector click that says "success" can be wrong, how to measure that, and how the Buddy System turns the problem into a checklist. This volume contains the project's most useful result so far, and it is a cautionary one.

7.1 The idea: a buddy for every photon

In the Buddy System, a computational photon is accompanied by ancillary ("buddy") photons that are prepared, used to help an operation, and then measured. The buddies are physical resources, not magic. The Buddy System is the proposed architecture for managing them in five stages: preparation, assistance, verification, preservation and continuation.

The key word is verification. In optics a gate often works only some of the time, and a helper detector click is supposed to tell us when. But a click is a detection, and detection is not the same as verification.

In plain words

A smoke alarm that goes off sometimes when there is no fire is not much use. A "success" signal that is sometimes wrong is the same: it tells you something happened, not that things are fine.

7.2 Before, Operation, After

Every operation is described in three parts.

PartQuestionExample
BeforeWhat state is prepared, what resources are needed, are they compatible?Two photons in addresses, plus two helper photons

7.3 Buddy Checks: PASS, FAIL or UNKNOWN

A Buddy Check is a test that decides whether it is safe to continue. It has three possible answers, on purpose:

  • PASS: demonstrated evidence says the operation worked.
  • FAIL: demonstrated evidence says it did not.
  • UNKNOWN: we do not have the evidence. This is the honest default.

An answer is never PASS just because a detector clicked.

7.4 Five different ways to say "it worked"

People often use one word, "success", for five different numbers. We keep them separate.

MeasurePlain meaning
Herald probabilityHow often does the success signal fire?
Herald reliabilityGiven that it fired, how often is the output actually valid?
Output fidelityGiven valid output, how close is the state to the target?
AvailabilityHow often is a usable photon even there?
Continuation yieldWhat fraction of starting runs end up with a usable result you can carry on with?
Common mistake

Common mistake. Quoting herald probability as if it were reliability. The R30 result below shows how large the gap can be.

Common mistake

Encoding-basis counts alone cannot certify quantum coherence or entanglement. Joint counts can show that two properties are statistically linked, but not whether the link is quantum. The states (00 + 01 + 10 + 11)/2 and (00 + 01 + 10 − 11)/2 give the same probability, 0.25, for every address. Yet the first is separable and the second is maximally non-separable (Volume 4, section 4.8). Reading out the Tetragon address counts therefore cannot tell them apart. The same holds for a pair of Tetragons (Volume 6, section 6.7): the joint address counts of an entangled pair are identical to those of a purely classical pairing with the same address matches, which has no entanglement at all. Certifying a correlation needs measurements in other bases, in effect a state characterization, and that uses up the photons it measures DERIVED. A PASS for a correlation therefore needs more evidence than the address counts alone, which is one more reason the default answer is UNKNOWN. Correct addresses and correct probabilities can still hide incorrect quantum information, and a destructive test that passes does not show that the photon can carry on into the next operation.

"Occupancy-valid" is a narrow word. It means exactly one photon in each computational output register. It is not state fidelity and it says nothing about whether coherence survived.

For other inputs the reliability can be 100%. So "1/3" is the worst case among the inputs studied, and 100% the best.

Key idea

In this reconstruction, for some inputs the success signal was wrong two times out of three. The herald was telling the truth about something, but not about what we needed.

What else we found

  • Each of the four (u,v) auxiliary outcome classes carries a quarter of the valid and a quarter of the false weight (E/4 and G/4). Keeping fewer outcome classes therefore does not improve the reliability in this model.
  • The false-herald operator is diagonal in the logical basis DERIVED. A false herald therefore doesn't scramble the addresses; it is an occupancy error, which a count of photons could in principle detect.
  • An independent numerical rerun passed the supplied 20 checks REPORTED. A rerun checks the code, not the lab.

What this does not say

  • It is not a claim about all analyzers or all detectors.
  • It is not a refutation of the published experiment. That experiment used post-selection on one photon per output port, and its reported overall success is 1/8 REPORTED. In that setting the occupancy is checked by detecting the photons, which consumes them.
  • It is not a measurement. SIMULATED only.

Memory, Loss and Survival

Why almost every number in this project is a loss number

What you will learn

Why almost every number in this project is a loss number, how the memory test (PK-M) decides whether a stored state is better than a classical guess, and what efficiency the memory would need.

8.1 What a quantum memory is for

A memory holds a photon's state for a while and gives it back on command. We need one to line up timing and to hold results while other steps happen. A memory is quantum only if it preserves amplitudes and phases (Volume 2), not just the "which address".

8.2 The simple test: beat the classical guess

If someone only measures the incoming photon and builds a replacement from the result, the best they can do has a known fidelity. A memory is certified if it does better than that. Different test sets have different classical limits, and we keep them apart:

BenchmarkValueApplies to
2/(d+1)0.40 for d = 4Hidden random pure states DERIVED

Joint classical bound for coherent states0.482253Coherent states with mean photon number 0.25, four independent modes DERIVED

The third is ((1 + n̄)/(1 + 2n̄))⁴ with n̄ = 0.25: (1.25/1.5)⁴ = (5/6)⁴ = 0.482253. The proof of this joint bound is UNVERIFIED: it is awaiting independent review.

Common mistake

Do not mix the benchmarks. A fidelity of 0.45 beats the 0.40 benchmark but not the 0.482253 one. Always say which test.

8.3 Four modes, four losses

In the memory design (PK-M) the four Tetragon addresses behave as four independent optical modes. Each mode suffers loss, and loss multiplies: the memory must be efficient enough that after loss and added noise, the state still beats the bound. In the model, the fidelity for a pure-loss memory with efficiency η and added thermal noise v per mode is

F = (1 + v + n̄ (1 − √η)²)⁻⁴

DERIVED for four independent Gaussian-ensemble modes. Two sanity checks:

  • If the memory returns nothing (η = 0, no noise) the fidelity is 0.4096, which is below the bound 0.482253, so an empty memory fails the test, as it should.
  • With no noise, the memory must pass a minimum efficiency (the threshold):
Mean photon number n̄Minimum efficiency (noiseless)

8.8 Known recipes versus unknown states

If a state was prepared by a known recipe and is lost, we can prepare it again. If an unknown state (the result of a computation) is lost, it cannot be rebuilt or copied. Memory loss is therefore much more costly for results than for inputs.

A06 asked: if we want to apply a change to many addresses, is it cheaper to use one shared operation, or to separate the addresses, change each one, and put them back? The operation was an H/V exchange applied to every twist mode, each mode left in place. It accounted for Before, Operation and After (Volume 7) and swept the number of modes, plate retardance drift, a rotation error and an element error.

  • When the state can stay where it is, the shared operation needs 1 part against 8 to 218 (for N = 2 to 32).
  • When the full twist must be read out afterwards, the saving is about 14 % (N = 2) to 20 % (N = 32) in parts counted.
  • Under the default assumptions the shared design keeps fidelity near 0.9996; the individual design falls to 0.99471 at N = 32, with transmission 0.937.
  • The shared design loses only if a real plate's retardance varies across modes by roughly 13 to 20 times the assumed amount. That is the first thing to measure.
  • Every error number is an assumption, the separating device is an illustrative model (published sorters are different devices), and preparation and detection were not simulated SIMULATED.

Volume 10

Intelligent Registers

A controller that composes only operations we can support

What you will learn

What a register is, three descriptive forms of register, and how a classical controller could pick the best supported sequence of operations. This volume is the most speculative in the series; everything here is PROPOSED.

10.1 What a register is

A register is an information-bearing system with a defined set of states. PhaseKeep describes three forms. They are descriptions, not three new devices.

FormMeaning
Binary RegisterA two-state encoding baseline. A coherent quantum version is a qubit; an ordinary classical bit is not a qubit.
Structured RegisterA fixed encoding arrangement, such as the four-state Tetragon.
Adaptive RegisterA register whose logical mapping or physical encoding can be selected or reconfigured within supported capabilities.
Common mistake

Simpler is not worse. A binary register is not inherently inferior. Choose the smallest representation that meets the goal. Several binary registers can also together form a larger system.

10.1b What a register description should record

Beyond the number of states, a register specification could list the properties the compiler needs PROPOSED: the EA contents, the address geometry, the state dimension, separability (can the axes be treated independently?), closure, symmetry (chances for a shared operation), the coherence requirements (which phases must survive) and physical compatibility (which parts can do the operation). The full table is in Volume 4, section 4.9.

The compiler can learn these from a known preparation record and from the mathematical description of the state. It cannot inspect an unknown photon and read them off.

10.2 Intelligent compilation

A classical controller (an ordinary computer) is given three things: a mathematical goal, a description of the register, and a characterized library of parts (parts whose behaviour has been measured). It then picks an EA mapping and a sequence of CT operations. It must

  • preserve the intended calculation, including relative phases,
  • count the cost of preparation, routing, readout and calibration.

Example: swapping H and V on the Tetragon can use one half-wave plate. But a swap applied only to one twist needs a different operation. The compiler cannot quietly replace the second by the first.

10.3 Intelligent execution

  1. Establish the intended logical operation and the accuracy required.
  2. Choose a supported register configuration and physical route.
  3. Check closure, compatibility, resources and expected loss.
  4. Configure and run the characterized CT operations.
  5. Use the available Buddy Checks to continue, retry where physically allowed, or stop.

Preparation records and equipment readings can guide adaptation today. Trustworthy live certification of a quantum state is a separate, unresolved problem (A04). A failed operation on an unknown state cannot necessarily be undone or retried.

10.4 A useful objective for the compiler

For each candidate sequence estimate: probability of useful completion, error, resource cost and delay. Compare only sequences that do the same logical task. Use measured data when it exists, and label modelled inputs ASSUMED. Reject unsupported operations rather than inventing a mechanism for them.

10.5 State space and the environment around it

Two ideas are easy to run together, so we separate them.

  • The State Space (SS) is the mathematical space of every available quantum state: the four addresses of the Tetragon and every coherent combination of them, with its amplitudes, phases and correlations. It says what information can exist.
  • The State Space Environment (SSE) is the physical resources and control that make that space accessible and useful. It says what the information can do, and unlike the abstract space it has losses, costs and timing.

In the vocabulary of Volume 4, the SS is the address geometry plus the state-space geometry (both mathematical). The SSE is the optical-mode geometry plus the execution geometry (both physical), together with the controller. It is a name for the whole stack seen together, not a new subsystem or pillar PROPOSED.

Three things can be changed during a computation: occupation (which addresses carry amplitude), connectivity (which addresses the machinery can couple coherently) and correlation (how the encoded properties, amplitudes and phases relate). They are distinct, though changing one can affect the others PROPOSED.

Three kinds of address set can also differ inside one environment: the addresses that are available, the addresses active in the current state (its support), and the relationships the physical devices can actually operate on. A register could have eight available addresses, four active ones and a transformation that acts on only two of them. Closure (Volume 5) is the question of whether the operations stay inside the available set.

Common mistake

Compare with the right thing. Against classical computing, configurable multilevel subspaces and encoding-aware compilation look new. Against quantum computing they are not: qudit encodings, subspace encodings, quantum control and hardware-aware compilation already exist. Any PhaseKeep contribution is in how these are combined for photons, not in inventing them.

10.6 Three corrections when reading the drawings

Pictures of squares, lines and triangles are useful, but three points keep them honest. They apply whenever the active set of addresses changes during a computation.

  1. The shape alone is not the state. The state (00 + 01 + 10 + 11)/2 and the state (00 + 01 + 10 − 11)/2 occupy the same four addresses and give the same measurement probabilities (0.25 each). Yet the first is separable (Schmidt weights 1 and 0) and the second is maximally non-separable (0.5 and 0.5). One sign changes the correlation structure without changing the picture DERIVED. A description must therefore record amplitudes and phases, not just which vertices are occupied.
  2. Occupied vertices are a set, not a shape of edges. Three occupied addresses are sometimes drawn as a triangle. But the square and cube graphs have no triangles: any two of three addresses on a square include a diagonal pair. The "triangle" is a group of addresses DERIVED.
  3. An unknown state cannot simply be squeezed. A reversible operation cannot concentrate an unknown four-address state into two addresses, because that would lose information. Contraction works only for a known class of states, or when the information is kept elsewhere. Dropping an occupied address by measuring or filtering is a loss DERIVED.

A related fact, checked by tetragon.py and set as an exercise in Volume 14: rotations in lines that share an address generally do not commute, so the order of operations matters, while rotations in disjoint lines do commute DERIVED.

10.7 Capability versus cost

The photon already has superposition, interference, phase and correlation. Geometry creates none of them. What a good arrangement might change is the cost of a task: two encodings with the same number of states can differ in the parts, loss and error needed for the same operation. A06 tested one narrow case of this (Volume 4, section 4.6). Whether the idea holds more widely is a hypothesis PROPOSED, and the test is to compare two encodings of the same size on the same task, counting parts, loss and error.

Common mistake

Capability versus cost. A register's computational capability is set by the operations it can reach, not by how its states are drawn. A geometric arrangement can only change the cost of an operation. Describing the evolution of a state as "a geometric operation" restates ordinary quantum evolution. A claimed advantage would have to be shown as a lower measured cost, and none has been.

10.8 What this does not claim

  • It does not claim a speedup. A classical controller choosing among the same operations establishes no advantage by itself, and geometric descriptions change cost, not capability.
  • It has not been built. Live adaptive quantum execution has not been demonstrated by PhaseKeep.
  • The only simulation relevant to it is the narrow A06 comparison of shared versus individual operations SIMULATED.
Evidence status

Evidence status. Adaptive register, intelligent compilation and the State Space Environment as a name for the stack: PROPOSED. The state examples in 10.6 and the non-commuting rotations: DERIVED. A06: SIMULATED with ASSUMED inputs. MEASURED: none.

Try it

Compare a two-state polarization register with a Tetragon for a task that only needs a polarization swap. Which is better? (The simpler one, if no twist information is needed. But if the twist carries information that matters, throwing it away is not a valid shortcut.)

Summary

Key idea

An adaptive register would let a controller choose how to encode and which supported operations to apply. Describing operations through lines, faces and shared structure may make them cheaper to compile, but it changes cost, not capability. It is an idea, not a demonstration, and it must reject operations that nothing can support.

11.1 A learning path

  1. Polarization with ordinary light. Malus's law says the intensity passing a second polarizer is I = I₀ cos²θ, where θ is the angle between the polarizers.
  2. Study spatial phase and twisted beams (OAM).
  3. Study mixing of modes and relative phase.
  4. Only then think about single photons and photon pairs.

11.3 The bench order for a real test

Start with an enclosed low-power coherent-light bench. Then:

  1. Characterize polarization and the ±1 modes.
  2. Measure waveplate retardance, axis, transmission and cross-mode behaviour.
  3. Test coherent superpositions, not just the basis states.
  4. Measure sorter crosstalk separately from transmission.
  5. Repeat with a fixed aperture and with a scaled aperture.
  6. Establish a real baseline before comparing costs.

Later: heralded sources, and two successive operations with a final characterization. Do not infer nondestructive verification from destructive data.

11.4 What a Tetragon bench test would show

A Tetragon test cannot show computing power, but it can show how cleanly and cheaply the basic operations can be done. These are plans, not results PROPOSED, and none has been done. In each case report the test states used, the fidelity against a stated benchmark, the loss, and survival and fidelity separately:

  • prepare chosen four-address pure states;
  • act on individual addresses and on selected pairs, and report crosstalk to the others;
  • apply one shared operation across equivalent lines, and report the part count and loss against doing each line separately;
  • create and remove non-separability between polarization and twist, with Schmidt weights found by a full state characterization;
  • keep coherence through successive operations;
  • as the first multi-photon target, produce two entangled Tetragons and report the entanglement by a full state characterization of the pair, with the loss counted. Pairs hyperentangled in polarization and OAM of ±1 have been produced by a source in the literature (Volume 6, section 6.7), so the first target is a reproduction and a measurement of cost, not a new physical effect. The pattern changes of Volume 6 can then be tried on photon B alone.

Non-separability between two properties of one photon is much easier to produce than entanglement between two photons, so it is a useful intermediate stage. It is not a substitute for the two-photon interactions of Volume 6, because a tie between two properties of one photon does not let one photon's operation depend on another photon's state. Bright (classical) light is the natural first test, with single photons later. Each result should also say which Buddy Check answer it earns: a result with no certification of the output stays UNKNOWN.

Common mistake

A four-level photon is easy to simulate. A single photon in a four-level system is described by four amplitudes, and any operation on it is a 4 × 4 matrix. A classical computer handles that instantly. So the Tetragon can test how cheaply and cleanly operations can be done, but it cannot show a computing advantage, whatever the geometry. The same goes for interference on one photon's modes: wave optics does that classically too. Any advantage claim must come from scaling and from multi-photon interaction, and it must be measured against the best classical alternative.

11.5 A fair comparison

Compare identical tasks with the same inputs and the same output requirements. Count preparation, parts, calibration, detection, failed attempts and readout. Report uncertainty and negative results. A numerical self-check tests the model, not the lab.

11.6 Open questions

  • Which published sorter gives a realistic baseline for A06?
  • How does waveplate retardance vary across real Laguerre-Gaussian modes, once common offsets are removed?
  • Which CT sequences keep the Tetragon intact, including superpositions?
  • Can a herald certify surviving occupancy without consuming the computational photons? (A04, BV-05)
  • Can an encoding-aware compiler beat a conventional one with matched resources?
  • Does the independent / coupled / entangling sorting of operations (Volume 6, section 6.7) predict the parts, loss and calibration needed for the same task, once measured?
  • Which device mixes twist −1 and +1 beyond a swap or a phase, and what are its loss and crosstalk?
  • Does the published apparatus match our reconstruction? (A02)
  • Can a timing-free Tetragon memory preserve relative phase? (B02)

11.7 What would prove us wrong

Good ideas say what would count against them. Here are things that would:

  • A published apparatus whose heralds are reliable in the very configuration we reconstructed (A02 would then show our model was incomplete).
  • A shared operation whose cost, measured on a real bench, is not lower than individually addressing the states.
  • A memory chain whose measured overall efficiency stays below the threshold of Volume 8.
  • A counterexample to the joint classical bound (B03).

11.8 Contribute a correction or an experiment

For simulations include code, random seed, dependency versions and raw output. For physical experiments include the apparatus, calibration, uncertainty and safety conditions. Do not send personal data or material you do not have the right to share. Negative results are welcome. A useful correction is a contribution, not a defeat.

We have a photon in address 10 (vertical polarization, twist −1). We want to (1) change it with ordinary optics, (2) make it interact with a second photon, (3) decide whether the interaction worked, and (4) store the result.

13.2 What these threads tell us about PhaseKeep

Heralding is the standard trade. The Knill-Laflamme-Milburn abstract says reliable operation needs feedback from detectors, and that without feedback the computation is non-deterministic. PhaseKeep's heralded gate (Volume 6) lives in this tradition: success is announced by detectors, and the whole question is whether the announcement can be trusted (Volume 7).

Higher dimensions are a recognized direction. The twisted-photon work treats OAM states as qudits. The Tetragon is a different design choice: four states built from two binary properties (polarization and a ±1 twist pair) rather than from many twist values on one property. That choice is the reason polarization and twist can be changed by separate devices (Volume 5).

Loss is quoted per operation. The fusion-based paper states its loss tolerance as a probability of loss per fusion (10.4 % for its ballistic scheme). That is the kind of metric that makes a scheme comparable and checkable.

Common mistake

Do not compare numbers across tasks. The 10.4 % figure is a loss tolerance for fusions in a specific architecture. PhaseKeep's 1.1146 % is an efficiency threshold for a specific memory test (Volume 8), and its 1/3 is a herald reliability in a specific model (Volume 7). They measure different things. Putting them side by side would prove nothing.

13.3 What PhaseKeep does and does not claim

  • It does not claim a quantum advantage, a speedup, or a working device.
  • Its proposed contributions are organizational: a way to name and count encodings (EA, GEA), a discipline for checking success (Buddy Checks), and a bookkeeping habit (evidence tags).
  • Whether this organization produces cheaper or more reliable operations is an open research question (Volume 11). The one small supporting result, the shared-waveplate comparison, is a simulation with assumed inputs.

13.4 How to read a new paper like a reviewer

The lessons of this library fit in a checklist. When a paper reports a gate, a memory or a source, ask:

  1. What is post-selected? What did the authors discard, and what fraction of attempts survives?
  2. What does "success" mean? Herald probability, reliability, output fidelity, availability or continuation yield (Volume 7)? Are they reported separately?
  3. Which fidelity, against which benchmark? A hidden random state, a known set of states, a process? (Volume 8)
  4. Is the output still available? Was the photon consumed to verify it?
  5. Is loss included? Source, coupling, detector and device loss all count.
  6. What is measured and what is modelled? Check each number's evidence class (Start Here).
  7. Can you rerun it? Is the code or data available, and does it reproduce? (Volume 9)
  8. What would change the conclusion? Which assumption, if wrong, breaks the result?

What you will learn

14.1 Run the numbers yourself

The code folder holds five programs. They need only Python 3 and nothing else (no NumPy, no internet). Open a terminal in the code folder and run:

python3 run_all.py

Each program checks its own results and prints ALL PASS, or stops with an error naming the failed check.

def fidelity(eta, v=0.0, nbar=0.25):
    return (1 + v + nbar * (1 - math.sqrt(eta)) ** 2) ** -4

Common mistake

A program that prints ALL PASS has confirmed the arithmetic and the model. It has not confirmed anything about a laboratory.

14.2 Exercises

Volumes 1 and 2: light and states

  1. Two ideal polarizers are 60° apart and the light entering the first is already polarized along it. What fraction of the intensity gets through the second? (Use Malus's law, I = I₀ cos²θ.)
  2. A twist −1 beam reflects off two mirrors. What is its twist afterwards?
  3. A photon is in the state (3|H⟩ + 4i|V⟩)/5. What are the probabilities of H and V?
  4. What is the best average fidelity of measure-and-resend for hidden random states in d = 3 and d = 8?
  5. How many real numbers specify a pure state of 8 levels?

Volumes 3 and 4: addresses and geometry

  1. Which addresses are H with twist +1 and V with twist +1?
  2. A device flips only polarization. Where does 11 go?
  3. For a register with 5 binary axes, how many corners, edges, square faces and cubes are there?
  4. Is (10 + 11)/√2 separable? What are its Schmidt weights?
  5. Is (00 + 10)/√2 separable?

Volumes 5 and 6: operations

  1. A photon in H, +1 meets a mirror and then a half-wave plate at 45°. Which address does it end in?
  2. An edge blend (|−1⟩ + |+1⟩)/√2 is rotated by φ = π/2. What is the new state, up to a global phase?
  3. Compute SUM on the pair (3, 2).
  4. What is the phase i^(jk) for j = 3, k = 2?
  5. Gates that each succeed with probability 1/8 are used five times in a row, all required to succeed, and the successes are independent. What is the overall probability?

Volumes 7 and 8: checking and memory

  1. With the reconstruction's probabilities, how many heralds, valid outputs and false heralds do you expect from 2,000 attempts?
  2. What is the joint classical bound for n̄ = 0.5, and the noiseless threshold efficiency?

  1. Which evidence tag fits "I reran the script and got the same output"? Which fits "the published paper reports 1/8"?

  7. A mirror turns 00 into 01 and 10 into 11. Why does one mirror suffice for both, and what does that save?
  8. Rotations are applied in the line {00, 01} and in the line {01, 11}. Do they commute? What if the lines are {00, 01} and {10, 11}?
  9. Why can a single Tetragon photon not demonstrate a computing advantage, whatever geometry is used?

  12. Why does the cyclic rotation pattern need an operation that couples polarization and twist on one photon?

Appendix 4

Math Refresher

Just enough complex numbers, vectors and matrices

Just enough to follow the "Go deeper" boxes. You never need this for the main text.

Complex numbers

A complex number has the form a + bi, where i is the square root of −1, so i² = −1. Its size is √(a² + b²). The powers of i cycle: i⁰ = 1, i¹ = i, i² = −1, i³ = −i, i⁴ = 1. That is why four-level systems use i: i^k repeats every four steps.

Probability from amplitude

If a state is a|H⟩ + b|V⟩, the probability of H is the squared size of a. The probabilities add to one.

Try it

a = 0.6 and b = 0.8. Probabilities? (0.36 and 0.64; they add to 1.)

Vectors and matrices

A state with d addresses is a column of d amplitudes. An operation is a d × d table of numbers (a matrix) that turns one column into another. Applying two operations in a row means multiplying their matrices.

The swap of H and V is the matrix with 0 on the diagonal and 1 off it, called X. The matrix Z has 1 and −1 on the diagonal. On four levels, Z|j⟩ = i^j|j⟩.

Tensor products

Two registers together are described by multiplying their state lists. Two four-level registers give 4 × 4 = 16 joint states |j,k⟩. An operation on one register alone is its matrix combined with the identity on the other.

Modular arithmetic

"(j + k) mod 4" means: add, then keep only the remainder after dividing by 4. So (2 + 3) mod 4 = 1. It is like a clock with four hours.

Powers and logarithm-free estimates

A loss of 5 % per step leaves 0.95 after one step and 0.95ⁿ after n steps. Survival multiplies, it does not add.

Binomial counts

C(n, k) ("n choose k") is the number of ways to pick k things from n: C(4, 2) = 6.

Fourier transform on four levels

The F matrix has entries i^(jk)/2. It turns the address basis into a "phase" basis. In Volume 6 it appears inside the construction of SUM.

Appendix 5

Questions and Misconceptions

Straight answers to the questions people ask first

Short answers to the questions people ask first. Each points to the volume with the full story.

Big picture

Is PhaseKeep a quantum computer? No. It is a research proposal. No PhaseKeep hardware has been built or measured. (Start Here)

Is it faster than a normal computer? Not established, and not claimed. A classical controller choosing which operations to apply does not by itself give an advantage. (Volumes 6 and 10)

What is actually new here? Mostly organization: EA (what carries information), GEA (how the states are arranged), CT (how they change), the Buddy System (how success is checked) and the evidence tags. Underlying physics is standard. Whether the organization helps is open. (Volume 13)

Who has checked this? The numbers have been recomputed by scripts, and the herald result was rerun by a second party. None of it has had independent peer review. (Volume 9)

Light and states

Is the square physical? The four corners are mathematical (a map of addresses). The beam shape, polarization pattern and the bench layout are physical. Four kinds of geometry are in play. (Volume 4, section 4.7)

Does the photon have four bits? No. It has four possible addresses, which carry two bits when read out directly, and one reading returns one address. The state is richer than any one reading, but you cannot read all of it. (Volumes 2 and 3)

Why can't you just copy a quantum state to protect it? The no-cloning theorem forbids copying an unknown state. Known recipes can be rebuilt; unknown results cannot. (Volume 2)

What is the difference between an edge blend and a diagonal blend? An edge blend can be written as one polarization times one twist blend; a diagonal blend cannot, and gives a beam whose polarization varies around the axis. (Volume 4, section 4.8)

Can a single four-level photon compute something a classical computer cannot? No. Four amplitudes and 4 × 4 operations are trivial to simulate. The Tetragon can test how cheaply operations are done, not computing power. Any advantage would have to come from scaling and multi-photon interaction. (Volume 11, section 11.4)

Why is high-dimensional entanglement the cornerstone? It is the quantum resource the other parts organize, change and protect, and the first target is two entangled Tetragons (up to 2 ebits). It is a project framing, not a result, and entanglement alone is not a computing advantage. (Start Here; Volume 6, section 6.7)

What is the State Space Environment? The physical resources and control that make the mathematical state space usable, with their losses, costs and timing. It is a name for the whole stack, not a new pillar. (Volume 10, section 10.5)

Is a line on the square the path a state travels? No. A line marks a two-address subspace. Inside it the amplitudes and relative phase can change continuously. (Volume 4, section 4.10)

Can geometry make a computation more powerful? It cannot add capability. It can change the cost of an operation if useful relationships line up with operations that are easy to perform. That is a hypothesis, tested only in one narrow simulation. (Volume 10, section 10.7)

Is "twist" the same as polarization? No. They are separate properties of the same light, which is why they can be combined as two axes. (Volume 1)

Operations and gates

Why does "controlled" not mean "conditional"? In PhaseKeep, a Controlled Transformation is one we can set and repeat. A conditional operation, one that depends on another qubit, is a kind of CT. (Volume 5)

Why do photons need helpers to interact? They ignore each other. Published gates use extra photons and keep only the attempts that look right. (Volume 6)

What does 1/8 mean? The published gate's theoretical success rate: 1/4 × 1/2. The rest of the attempts are discarded. (Volume 6)

What is a false herald? A success signal that fires when the output is not valid. In our reconstruction it happens 0.25 of the time, against 0.125 for valid outputs. (Volume 7)

Checking and memory

What does PASS / FAIL / UNKNOWN mean? PASS needs demonstrated evidence, FAIL needs demonstrated evidence the other way, and UNKNOWN is the honest default. A detector click is not evidence of success by itself. (Volume 7)

Does the false-herald result mean the published experiment is wrong? No. It describes our reconstruction with ideal detectors. The published experiment relies on post-selection, and the mapping to its apparatus is still pending (A02). (Volume 7)

What is "occupancy-valid"? Exactly one photon in each computational output register. It is not state fidelity. (Volume 7)

Why is almost every number a loss number? Because photons are lost at every device, and loss multiplies along the chain. (Volume 8)

Why are there three different benchmark numbers (0.40, 0.25, 0.482253)? They belong to different tests: hidden random states, an entangled target, and a known set of coherent states. Do not mix them. (Volume 8)

Using this library

How do I check a number? Look it up in the Ledger (Appendix 2), then run the matching program in the code folder. (Volume 14)

What do the coloured tags mean? They say how much weight a claim bears: DERIVED, REPORTED, SIMULATED, ASSUMED, PROPOSED, MEASURED (none yet), UNVERIFIED, BLOCKED. (Start Here)

How can I contribute a correction? Send the exact claim, its tag, the assumptions, the derivation or source and a reproducible procedure. Negative results are welcome. (Volume 11)

Naming decisions in force

  • EA (Encoding Axes, "Axies") and CT (Controlled Transformation) are the working terms. PTEA and TV are retired.
  • GEA and the Buddy System are the two pillars. High-dimensional entanglement is the adopted scientific cornerstone beneath them.
  • State Space Environment (SSE) is a descriptive term for the whole physical-plus-control stack. It is not a pillar. Dynamic State Geometry (DSG) was suggested as a name for evolving geometric structures and is not used in this library.
  • PQGS (Photonic Quantum Geometric Superpositions) is a descriptive term inside GEA. It is not a third pillar, and "geometric" does not mean geometric phase.
  • Time is an external clock in the current design, not an EA.
